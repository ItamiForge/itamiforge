I am an engineer by training and inclination, with close to a decade of experience working in analytics, data systems, and applied machine learning. A varied experience across multiple industries, most recently in large-scale brewing and manufacturing environments. Over the years, what has shaped my perspective most is not exposure to newer tools, but repeated exposure to real operational problems—and the gap between how they are marketed versus how they are best solved.

## The Dilution of Objectives

Across roles and organizations, including time spent working within one of the world’s largest brewing setups, I have seen firsthand how easily objectives get diluted. The focus often shifts from solving a clearly defined process problem to engineering a complex solution because modern tools make it possible, not because they are necessary. Cloud platforms, large AI models, and packaged enterprise stacks are frequently introduced early, long before the problem itself is fully understood.

## Grounded Solutions

In manufacturing and supply-chain contexts, many high-impact problems—yield variability, process stability, quality deviations, energy efficiency—are grounded in physics, chemistry, and control systems. They are often better addressed through disciplined data collection, strong domain understanding, and classical analytical methods that are transparent, testable, and explainable.

In my experience, simple and well-designed models deployed close to the process, using clean and relevant data, can outperform far more expensive and elaborate systems built around generalized platforms and top-tier AI models.

## The Right Role for AI

This is not a rejection of AI or machine learning. I have built and led such systems myself. But I am cautious about how and where they are applied. In many industrial settings today, the economic return from large, cloud-dependent models does not justify their cost or operational complexity.

Lightweight, narrowly scoped models—used as interfaces, interpreters, or assistants rather than decision-makers—tend to add far more value when paired with solid engineering and domain expertise.

## Open Systems and Ownership

I am also a strong proponent of open systems and long-term ownership. Wherever possible, I prefer solutions that minimize licensing dependencies, avoid unnecessary data movement, and are understandable and maintainable by the client’s own teams. My objective has always been to leave behind systems that are simple to reason about, modular in design, and well documented—systems that can evolve without permanent reliance on external vendors.

## Moving Forward

After stepping away from full-time consulting and analytics leadership, I now engage selectively as a technical partner and advisor. My interest is in working with breweries, engineering firms, and operators who value clarity over novelty, and outcomes over presentation. The work I take on is focused on problem definition, architecture, analytics, and practical deployment—grounded in engineering reality rather than technology fashion.
